Artifact 3

Bias Detection and Responsible AI Leadership

Demonstrates the ability to identify, audit, and mitigate human bias in AI/ML systems and articulate leadership responsibilities when bias enters data pipelines, algorithms, and deployment decisions at enterprise scale.

This artifact is not a compliance checklist. It frames fairness as an ongoing engineering discipline: a practice of asking how system choices, data, evaluation methods, and organizational incentives affect people before and after deployment.

Five bias entry points

  • Data selection: historical data can exclude, underrepresent, or encode unequal past decisions.
  • Labeling: labels may reflect subjective judgments or inconsistent standards.
  • Algorithm design: objectives and proxy variables can create unequal effects across groups.
  • Testing and evaluation: aggregate metrics can hide weaker performance for subgroups.
  • Interpretation of results: teams can over-trust outputs or misuse predictions outside their intended context.

Leadership practices

  • Maintain decision traceability from data selection through deployment.
  • Separate intuition from validation through documented evidence and tests.
  • Introduce deliberate review points before high-impact releases.
  • Review data representation before it enters the pipeline.
  • Use cross-functional review structures that include technical, domain, and affected-user perspectives.

Measurable outcomes to track: subgroup performance differences, representation coverage, number of identified-and-resolved issues, review completion rate, drift alerts, and time to remediate a bias finding.

Course alignment: Articulate successful AI/ML integration with attention to ethical leadership, bias mitigation, data governance, and human judgment.

References

  • Barocas, S., Hardt, M., & Narayanan, A. (2023). Fairness and machine learning: Limitations and opportunities. https://fairmlbook.org
  • National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2023). AI Risk Management Framework 1.0. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1
  • O'Neil, C. (2016). Weapons of math destruction. Crown.
Artifact 4

Communicating Neural Networks to Non-Technical Audiences

Demonstrates the ability to translate complex AI/ML concepts into clear, audience-appropriate communication—a core competency for leaders integrating machine learning into organizational change initiatives.

The ball-catching analogy

Imagine teaching someone to catch a ball. At first, they miss because they do not yet know how far away it is, how fast it is moving, or when to raise their hands. After each attempt, they notice the error and adjust: move sooner, raise their hands higher, or shift left or right. Over many attempts, they improve.

A neural network learns in a similar way. It receives inputs, makes a prediction, compares that prediction with the correct answer, measures the error, and adjusts its internal weights. Repeating this process across many examples helps it recognize patterns. Like a ball catcher, however, it can only learn well from examples that represent the situations it will face.

  • Input layer: what the system observes, like the ball's direction and speed.
  • Hidden layers: internal pattern detection, like learning when and where to move.
  • Output: the prediction or decision, like attempting the catch.
  • Backpropagation: learning from the miss and adjusting for the next attempt.

Leadership limitation: A neural network does not understand context, fairness, or consequences on its own. Its performance depends on data quality, its training objective, and the conditions under which it is used. Low-confidence or high-impact decisions require human review.

Measurable outcomes to track: audience comprehension feedback, accuracy of the explanation when repeated by participants, appropriate escalation of low-confidence outputs, and model performance across real operating conditions.

Course alignment: Apply communication strategies that support organizational learning and change leadership in AI/ML integration.

References

  • Goodfellow, I., Bengio, Y., & Courville, A. (2016). Deep learning. MIT Press.
  • Molnar, C. (2022). Interpretable machine learning (2nd ed.). https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/
